# Reviewed coaching techniques

Reviewed 11 November 2026 for issue #20. These sources support bounded coaching suggestions, not a claim that this bot is clinically validated or that one method works for everyone. The product has no age cutoff. Population limits below constrain what the coach may claim; they do not determine who may participate.

| Technique | When to offer it | Evidence and limits | Example product suggestion |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Choose a feasible action and repeat it around a familiar cue | A participant is starting or finds the current action too difficult | Singh et al. reviewed 20 health-behaviour studies involving 2,601 participants; study mean ages ranged from 21.5 to 73.5. Habit formation varied substantially, and 11 studies had high risk of bias. Self-selected behaviours and repetition/timing were relevant to habit strength. This does not establish an optimal tiny-action size or prove effectiveness for children's learning habits. [Review](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/39685110/) | “Choose a reading step you feel able to repeat, perhaps one page after breakfast.” The one-page size and reading context are practical suggestions, not a tested prescription. |
| Make an if–then plan | The participant wants to act but forgets or has no clear opportunity | Gollwitzer and Sheeran's meta-analysis synthesised 94 independent tests of implementation intentions: plans linking a situation to a chosen response helped goal attainment on average. This is evidence for planning, not a guarantee of automatic habit formation or the same effect at every age. [Authors' university record and paper](https://www.socmot.uni-konstanz.de/publications/implementation-intentions-and-goal-achievement-meta-analysis-effects-and-processes), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0065-2601(06)38002-1) | “If I finish dinner, then I will practise one guitar chord.” Ask the participant to choose the cue and action rather than impose a schedule. |
| Record progress and use it to adjust | A participant wants to understand consistency or reflect on obstacles | Harkin et al. synthesised 138 randomised studies involving 19,951 participants. Interventions increasing progress monitoring improved goal attainment on average. Effects varied with study quality and context. Reported/public and recorded monitoring had larger effects in moderator analyses; this does not justify forced disclosure or prove that public posting is best for a particular child or adult. [Publisher's full paper](https://www.apa.org/pubs/journals/releases/bul-bul0000025.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1037/bul0000025) | “Record today's result, then notice what made starting easier.” Keep group sharing optional; explain that progress requested inside a group is visible there. |
| Offer optional social comparison cautiously | A participant explicitly enjoys friendly competition | Zhang et al.'s 11-week trial involved 790 university students (mean age 25.2) and rewarded exercise-class attendance. Comparison conditions increased attendance in that setting. The authors warn against generalising from one university's young adults; this does not validate family leaderboards, children's outcomes, lasting habits, or emotional wellbeing. [Authors' university-hosted paper](https://ndg.asc.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/Support-or-Competition-pdf.pdf), [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.pmedr.2016.08.008) | “Would an optional consistency challenge feel encouraging?” Participation, sharing and withdrawal remain separate choices. No shame, forced ranking, or pressure to increase unsafe effort. |

## How to select advice

Start with the participant's stated goal, interests, available time, ability and preferences. If an essential detail is missing, ask one simple question. Offer one practical next step rather than a catalogue of techniques. Do not infer limitations, maturity or interests from age alone. Use familiar wording and a feasible action based on what the participant tells the coach.

The coach may offer an easier version after a missed day, but must label it as a practical adaptation. None of these references establishes that a particular two-minute rule, streak length or fixed number of days guarantees success. A stored streak measures reported consistency; it does not measure psychological automaticity or independently verify completion.

There is no separate youth-effectiveness claim in this catalog. For a participant of any age, general suggestions remain suggestions; do not translate adult health-study findings into claims of proven effects for a child's schoolwork or an older adult's abilities. Do not prescribe treatment or create health/fitness challenges from these papers.

## Competition is a product experiment

The current weekly score counts completed days of one chosen habit, not volume. This design is intended to reduce pressure to outperform another person's quantity; it is not directly validated by the trial above. Habits differ in difficulty, and scores must never be described as ability, effort, character or worth. If a participant finds comparison discouraging, suggest leaving the challenge or turning off sharing and focus on their own progress.

## Review and evaluation

The references are a small reviewed catalog, not an exhaustive systematic review performed by this project. The model must not fabricate additional citations or numerical effects. Use the linked paper when a participant asks for the basis of a technique, and explain the relevant limitation concisely.

Extend `docs/pilot-acceptance.md` with observations from actual pilot use: understandable language, feasible suggestions, consent respected, no shame, no unsupported guarantees, and no false claims of saving progress. Prompt instructions and passing software tests do not establish coaching effectiveness. New evidence or broader claims require another source review.
